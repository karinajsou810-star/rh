What more than seven years running one busy office taught me about why good people leave, and why they stay.

For more than seven years, I ran the day to day of a busy executive office. Schedules, travel, service providers, events and a small team where someone's whole day depended on the details being right. Nobody there called it people operations. It was simply the work. This article is what that work taught me about why good people leave growing teams, and what quietly convinces them to stay.

Where this comes from

Hotels and busy homes taught me one thing: care only works when there is structure behind it.

Before the digital world, I worked in hospitality, personal assistance, and people operations. Hotels, busy households, an executive office. Different settings, and the same lesson in every one of them.

A hotel morning runs on the housekeeping plan, so the front desk can welcome people by name. A busy household runs on routines, so the family can simply live their day instead of managing it. An executive office runs on structure, so the day arrives prepared and nothing depends on luck. Behind the care people feel, there are always lists, plans, and routines, kept in order by someone who takes that work seriously.

Growing teams are no different. The founders I meet care deeply about their people. What they are missing is not heart. It is the structure that lets the care show.

When nothing is written down

When nothing is written down, your best people carry everything.

In a growing team, hiring, onboarding, schedules, service providers and client work all run at the same time, usually on top of someone's already full day. It can quickly become overwhelming. Priorities change by the hour. New people learn by following whoever has time to explain. Every answer lives in someone's head, and everything feels urgent because nothing was planned far enough ahead to be anything else.

Here is what I learned watching routines like this from the inside: the cost does not show up as a missed deadline. It shows up quietly, in your best people. They are the ones who hold the day together, who remember what nobody wrote down, who fix what nobody saw breaking. They cover for it quietly, and from the outside everything looks fine. Until one of them hands in their notice, and you discover how much was resting on them.

30–200%

And beyond the number, there is the part no spreadsheet shows: the knowledge that leaves with them, the clients who feel the difference, and the questions the rest of the team starts asking themselves when a good person walks out the door.

Why your best people leave

They rarely leave for money. They leave because something was missing long before they handed in their notice.

I watched it up close for years: the decision to leave is made months before the notice. Exit interviews are almost always too late and too polite to surface the real reasons. People default to “a better opportunity” because it is the polite answer. The actual drivers are more specific, and more preventable.

Randstad's Country Report Portugal (2019) asked Portuguese workers directly what leads them to stay and what pushes them to leave. The results are telling.

Compensation matters, but it is the floor, not the ceiling. The factors that keep people sit above it: security, environment, recognition, growth. When those are missing, a competitive salary is rarely enough to hold someone who has options. And in a disorganized routine, they are always the first things to go missing.

A McKinsey study (2004) found

Factors such as a stimulating work environment, learning opportunities, and a sense of belonging to a team of like-minded people were rated more important by workers than a high salary. A good compensation is expected. What makes people stay, or leave, is everything around it.

Source: Kalyankar, Mathur & Bakshi (2014), citing McKinsey (2004)

Structure is care

Structure is care. Your team feels the difference every single day.

I did not learn this in an HR course. I learned it printing itineraries at six in the morning, planning coverage before the holidays arrived, and writing down routines so a new colleague would never have to learn by guessing.

Because that is what structure says to a team, without saying a word. A documented routine says: your day is not left to chance. A real welcome plan says: we were expecting you. Planned coverage says: you can be away, and nothing will land on you when you return. A regular check-in says: we see you. None of this appears in a benefits package. All of it is retention.

What structure feels like from inside a team

You know what is expected of you this week. You know who owns what, so you ask once and get an answer. Your first day was prepared before you arrived. Your holiday does not create a crisis. And when you do good work, someone notices, because noticing is on the calendar too.

People rarely describe this as “good processes.” They describe it as “a place where I feel taken care of.”

This is what I mean when I say I turn care into structure. Not bureaucracy, and not systems for their own sake. The daily, visible proof that someone is looking after the people and the details at the same time.

Start smaller than you think

You do not need a transformation project. You need one thing implemented at a time.

The founders I meet are not indifferent to their people. They are overwhelmed, and the people side keeps getting pushed to next quarter. Until someone resigns, and it becomes urgent on the worst possible day.

The good news is that the fix is smaller than it looks. One shared calendar. One welcome plan for the next person who joins. One weekly rhythm where updates are collected and decisions get made. Implemented one at a time, in weeks, not quarters. That is where hands-on support comes in: someone who has run routines like this for years, doing it with you, not handing you a report about it.

What this looks like in practice

In a private executive office, continuity depended on a small number of experienced people. Extended leave could expose gaps in routine knowledge, service-provider information and confidence about when to escalate.

I used approximately three days of practical onboarding to prepare two trusted recurring backup resources. The combination of supervised practice, clear handovers and realistic coverage planning supported absences of up to approximately 30 days.

After more than seven years of keeping one demanding routine running, I can tell you where it always starts: not with a framework, but with a simple question. What does your team experience every day, compared with what you intend? That gap is where good people are lost. And it is where they are kept. It is not a matter of effort. It is what happens when a team grows faster than its structure, and the fix is closer than it looks.
